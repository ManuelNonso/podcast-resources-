Before You Begin

How to Use This Worksheet

This is the coaching session the episode prepared you for — in written form, with room to go further than a single listening allows. Four parts. Nine exercises. One event named honestly, and one training step taken toward it by the end.

The parts move in sequence. Part One is where you separate the event you were actually built for from the one you've been playing instead. Part Two turns that clarity into a training plan. Part Three pressure tests the plan against the exact moments most likely to pull you back into someone else's event. Part Four makes it a practice.

Write what is actually true, not the version that honors whoever's expectation you've been fulfilling. Naming whose voice put you in the wrong event is not blame. It is the first honest step back toward your own.

🏅 The Olympics — Hold This Throughout

The javelin thrower is never competing against the marathoner. Different training, different bodies, different measure of victory. And if they each win their event, they're both called exactly the same thing: champion.

Your life works the same way. This worksheet is how you find out which event you were actually trained for, and what it's costing you to keep playing someone else's instead.

What This Session Will Produce
  • Something named — the specific event you've actually been trained for, based on real evidence from your own life
  • Something traced — whose voice first put you in a different event, and why it made sense at the time
  • Something separated — your worth from their event, and your event from being a lesser one
  • Something committed — one specific training step toward your own event, taken within the next seven days
Part One

Discover — Seeing Which Event Is Actually Yours

Before you can retrain for your own event, you need to see clearly which one you've actually been running, and whose voice put you there.

1
The Event Audit — Trained For vs Playing In
Separating what you were built for from what you've actually been running.

This exercise begins with an honest inventory. Not the version that justifies the path you're currently on. The real one.

Coaching Prompt

What has come naturally to you, quietly and consistently, for as long as you can remember, even when nobody was applauding it? And what event are you currently pouring your real effort into instead?

Not what you were told to pursue. What you were quietly built for. Where does the gap between the two land in your body?

Notice ThisIf the effort in your second column feels disproportionate to the results, more exhausting than it should be for how much you're actually achieving, that mismatch is often the clearest evidence of the wrong event.

2
The Origin — Whose Voice Put You There
Tracing who first decided which event was worth pursuing.

Playing the wrong event is not a personal failure. It was usually shaped by a specific voice, at a specific time, for reasons that made sense then. You cannot retrain fully until you've traced it.

Coaching Prompt

Whose voice first put you in the event you've actually been running? A parent, a partner, a teacher, a culture. When did you first learn that your own natural event wasn't good enough, impressive enough, or safe enough to pursue on its own terms?

Go back to the earliest version of this, not the most recent pressure. What was the specific moment or season this decision was made?

Notice ThisNaming whose voice this was is not about blame. That voice likely believed it was protecting you. It simply wasn't trained to see the event you were actually built for.

3
The Injury Audit — Naming the Real Cost
Seeing plainly what playing the wrong event has actually cost you.

In Episode 52, an athlete filled in for a friend's event and sustained an injury that cost her recovery time in her own. This exercise asks you to name your own injury plainly.

Coaching Prompt

What is the specific cost you are currently carrying from playing the wrong event? Not a vague dissatisfaction. The actual injury.

Exhaustion that rest doesn't fix. Resentment toward people who seem to move easily. A quiet sense of always falling short despite trying hard. Name it specifically.

"That injury didn't just cost her the high jump competition. It affected her own game. She had to recover, which meant time away from the event she was actually built to excel in."
Part Two

Define — Building Your Way Back to Your Own Event

Part Two turns what you named in Part One into a working plan: your actual event, the evidence behind it, and your first training step back toward it.

4
Finding Your Event — Your Working Builder
One page. Your event, your evidence, your first training step.

This is the working document everything else refers back to. Keep it specific. A vague sense of "purpose" is much harder to train for than a named event.

5
The Words — Releasing the Comparison
Drafting the sentence that separates your worth from their event.

Comparison will very likely return the moment you start training publicly for your own event. Writing the response now makes it far more available when you need it.

Coaching Prompt

If you had to say it in one breath, what is the sentence that reminds you your event was never a lesser one, no matter how it compares to someone else's?

Write it the way you'd actually say it to yourself in the moment of comparison, not the way it would sound on a poster.

Read it back out loud once before moving on. If it steadies you rather than convinces you, it's ready.

Part Three

Pressure Test — Testing the Plan Against Real Life

A training plan survives easily in writing. It does not survive as easily against disappointed expectations, an injury or setback, or a smaller-looking event, unless you've already thought it through.

6
Scenario One — The Disappointed Expectation
When the voice from Exercise 2 pushes back.
The Scenario

You begin training visibly for your own event. The parent, partner, or teacher whose voice originally shaped your path notices, and responds with disappointment, confusion, or pressure to return to the event they expected of you.

7
Scenario Two — A Setback in Your Own Event
When training for your real event doesn't go smoothly either.
The Scenario

You take the training step from Exercise 4, and it's harder than expected, or it doesn't go well. It would be easy to conclude that even your own event isn't actually yours after all.

Coaching Prompt

What is the difference between a setback in early training and evidence that this isn't your event?

Every athlete has rough training sessions in their own event. A difficult start is not the same as being in the wrong race.

8
Scenario Three — When Your Event Looks Smaller
When your calling doesn't look as impressive from the outside.
The Scenario

Your actual event doesn't come with the same visible prestige as the one you left behind, or the one someone else is running. It would be easy to quietly downgrade your own effort because the event itself looks smaller from the stands.

Part Four

Protect — Making This a Practice, Not a Moment

One training step is a good start. It is not the whole shift. Part Four turns this worksheet into an ongoing practice, so you catch yourself quickly the next time you drift back into someone else's event.

9
The Weekly Training Check — Building the Habit
The simple, recurring practice that keeps you in your own event.

The goal is not to make one correction and stop. The goal is to build the habit of noticing quickly when you've drifted back into training for someone else's event.

  • Within 7 Days: Take the training step named in Exercise 4, by the deadline you set, whether or not it feels impressive yet.
  • The Day After: Write down what actually happened, and compare it honestly to what fear or comparison predicted.
  • Every Week: Ask yourself one question. "Whose event have I been training for this week, mine or someone else's?"
  • Every Month: Revisit your Event Audit from Exercise 1. Has anything shifted? Update it honestly.
  • Whenever Comparison Hits: Return to the words from Exercise 5 before letting someone else's event convince you yours is lesser.

Before You Close This

The athlete who filled in for her friend wasn't wrong to help, and her friend wasn't wrong to ask. That's exactly what makes this so easy to miss in our own lives. Playing someone else's event rarely comes from a bad place. It usually comes from love, loyalty, or simply not knowing there was ever another option.

But knowing better now means you get to choose differently. Your event is still there, waiting for your training, no matter how long you've been out on someone else's field.

Living in Full Expression means discovering which part of life's Olympic Games you were called to play, and giving it everything you have, regardless of how big or small it looks from the outside.

Before you close this session — write the one thing you now know, that you did not know when you opened it.

The other event will still be there, still getting its applause, still looking impressive from the stands. You now know exactly which event is actually yours, exactly what it's cost you to play someone else's, and exactly what your first step back looks like. The only thing left is the seven days.

Go train for your own event this week.
