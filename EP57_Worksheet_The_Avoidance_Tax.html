Before You Begin

How to Use This Worksheet

This is the coaching session the episode prepared you for, in written form, with room to go further than a single listening allows. Four parts. Nine exercises. One honest diagnosis, and one first move, taken by the end of it.

This worksheet is not only about exercise. It is about the specific pain hiding inside any action you already know matters and have not gone back to, whether that shows up in your health, your work, your relationships, your finances, or your own growth. Pick the one costing you the most right now, and work that one all the way through.

From Part Two onward, this worksheet branches into two paths, physical or practical pain, and exposure or social pain, because they are not the same problem and they are not solved the same way. Read both branches before choosing. Many people find their real pattern is not the one they expected.

🧅 The Onion — Hold This Throughout

Most people try to change from the outer layer. That is the goal itself, the plan, the promise to try harder next time. That layer is not where the resistance actually lives.

The layer that actually stings is buried further in, underneath the goal, underneath the plan, sitting quietly as a specific discomfort you have been avoiding without ever naming it directly. This worksheet is how you peel down to that layer, on purpose, instead of managing the outer one for another year.

What This Session Will Produce
  • Something named — the specific action you have been avoiding, and the domain of your life where it actually lives
  • Something traced — where you first learned that this kind of discomfort was something to avoid rather than move through
  • Something separated — the pain you are protecting yourself from, from your actual capacity to move through it
  • Something committed — one first move, with a deadline and a person watching, taken within the next seven days
Part One

Discover — Naming What You're Actually Avoiding

Before you can act, you need to see clearly which action this is, how long it has actually been sitting untouched, and what kind of pain is really keeping it there.

1
The Domain Scan
Naming the specific action, and where it actually lives in your life.

This exercise begins with an honest inventory. Not the version that makes the delay sound reasonable. The real one.

Coaching Prompt

What is the action you already know matters, and have not gone back to, or never started? Where does it actually live: your health, your work, a relationship, your finances, or your own growth?

Name it specifically. Then be honest about how long it has actually been sitting there. Weeks. Months. Years. Where does that real number land in your chest?

How Long This Has Actually Been Sitting There

Notice ThisIf someone else has already commented on this gap before you named it to yourself, that is often a sign the avoidance has been running longer than you have been willing to admit.

2
The Origin — Tracing Where the Avoidance Started
Finding the earliest root, whichever kind of pain it turns out to be.

Avoidance is not a character flaw. It was formed somewhere specific, around a specific kind of discomfort. You cannot address a root you have not traced.

Coaching Prompt

Think about the last time you actually tried to begin this action. What happened, in your body or your mind, in the very first few minutes? When did you first learn that this exact feeling was something to avoid, rather than something to move through?

Sit with the actual feeling rather than the explanation you have gotten used to giving. Trace it back to the earliest time you remember feeling this exact way.

Notice ThisBoth kinds of pain are legitimate. Physical or practical discomfort was formed to protect your body and your energy. Exposure or social pain was formed to protect your standing and your sense of safety with others. Neither one is weakness.

3
The Diagnosis — Physical Pain or Exposure Pain
Naming plainly which kind of pain you're actually avoiding.

This is the single most important exercise in this worksheet. Getting the diagnosis right changes everything that follows. Don't rush it.

Coaching Prompt

When you picture yourself starting, is the discomfort mostly physical or practical, effort, fatigue, inconvenience, or is it mostly about being seen, judged, or found lacking by someone else?

If it is mostly bodily or logistical, this is likely physical or practical pain. If it is mostly about how you will be perceived, this is likely exposure or social pain. Many people carry a mix. Name whichever is stronger.

"You are not avoiding the action. You are avoiding a specific pain hiding inside it, and willpower cannot fix a threat it has never identified."

5
The Words — Drafting What You'll Tell Yourself
Preparing the sentence for the moment the discomfort returns.

The discomfort will very likely return once you take this step. Writing the response now, while you're clear headed, makes it far more available in the moment you actually need it.

Coaching Prompt

What is the one sentence that reminds you the return of the discomfort is not a sign to stop, it is a sign you are exactly where the work happens?

Write it the way you would actually say it to yourself, not the way it would sound on a poster.

Read it back out loud once before moving on. If it sounds like something you'd tell a client or a friend you love, it's ready.

Part Three

Pressure Test — Testing the Plan Against Real Life

A plan survives easily in writing. It does not survive as easily under comparison, a stalled result, or a real slip, unless you've already thought it through. Part Three does that thinking now.

6
Scenario One — Nobody Seems to Struggle With This But You
When comparison makes the avoidance feel like a personal flaw.
The Scenario

You notice someone who seems to do this action easily, without any of the resistance you feel. The instinct is to conclude you are simply wired wrong, when really you are only seeing their outer layer, not the pain they have already peeled through.

7
Scenario Two — The Discomfort Doesn't Ease When It's Supposed To
When the pain outlasts the timeline you expected.
The Scenario

You take the first rep or rehearsal from Exercise 4, and the discomfort does not fade as quickly as you hoped. It would be easy to conclude this proves the whole thing was a mistake.

8
Scenario Three — You Slip Back After Real Progress
When the old story returns after weeks of doing well.
The Scenario

You keep the action going for two solid weeks, then life happens and you miss several days in a row. The old story starts telling you this is exactly what always happens, and you might as well stop again.

Part Four

Protect — Making This a Practice, Not a Moment

One first move is a good start. It is not the whole shift. Part Four turns this worksheet into an ongoing practice, so the next avoidance does not get to run quietly for months before you notice it.

9
The Weekly Practice — Building the Habit
The simple, recurring practice that catches avoidance early next time.

The goal is not to get one action right. The goal is to build the habit of noticing quickly when you are avoiding a pain instead of an action.

  • Within 7 Days: Take the first rep or rehearsal named in Exercise 4, by the deadline you set, whether or not the discomfort has eased.
  • The Day After: Write down what actually happened, and compare it honestly to what the pain predicted.
  • Every Week: Ask yourself one question. "What am I avoiding this week that I've been calling 'not the right time'?"
  • Every Month: Revisit your Domain Scan from Exercise 1. Has anything new started sitting untouched? Name it while it's still fresh.
  • Whenever Comparison Hits: Run the reminder from Exercise 6 before letting someone else's ease convince you that you are simply wired wrong.

Before You Close This

My legs ached for three days after I went back. On day two, that ache tried to talk me out of continuing, using the exact same voice that had kept me away in the first place. The only difference the second time was that I could see it clearly enough to keep going anyway.

Whatever your action is, the pain hiding inside it was never going to disappear by waiting a little longer, or by knowing more about why the action matters. It moves the moment you name it precisely and build a structure around that truth instead of around good intentions.

Living in Full Expression means telling the truth about what you have been avoiding, and refusing to let an unnamed pain quietly run a decision that belongs to you.

Before you close this session — write the one thing you now know, that you did not know when you opened it.

The pain will keep hiding in that action whether you name it or not. You now know exactly which action it is, exactly which kind of pain it is, and exactly what your first honest move looks like. The only thing left is the seven days.

Name the pain before it names your life.
Live in full expression.
