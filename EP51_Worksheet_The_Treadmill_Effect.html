Before You Begin

How to Use This Worksheet

This is the coaching session the episode prepared you for — in written form, with room to go further than a single listening allows. Four parts. Nine exercises. One treadmill named honestly, and one real-ground step you will have actually taken by the end of it.

The parts move in sequence. Part One is where you get honest about what's actually a dashboard number in your life, and what's real ground. Part Two turns that honesty into a plan. Part Three pressure tests the plan against the exact moments most likely to put you back on the machine. Part Four makes it a practice, not a one-time insight.

Write what is actually true, not the version that makes the busyness sound reasonable. The fear you name here does not need to be resolved before you finish this worksheet. It only needs to be named clearly enough that it stops running the show without your permission.

🏃 The Treadmill vs the Open Road — Hold This Throughout

On a treadmill, you sweat exactly as hard as you would outside. Your legs get exactly as tired. The screen even gives you a number, distance covered. None of it is real. You never leave the spot, never feel the wind change, never see anything new.

Outside, the ground is uneven. The weather is unpredictable. You might trip. That risk is also the only way you actually arrive anywhere. This worksheet is how you find out which one you've actually been running on.

What This Session Will Produce
  • Something named — the specific activity in your life that produces sweat but no real distance
  • Something traced — the specific fear that activity has been quietly protecting you from
  • Something separated — the difference between failing at something and being a failure, based on your own evidence
  • Something committed — one real-ground step, with a deadline, taken while the fear is still present
Part One

Discover — Seeing Your Own Treadmill

Before you can get off the machine, you need to see clearly that you're on it. Part One separates real ground from dashboard numbers, and traces where the underlying fear first took hold.

1
The Dashboard Audit — Sweat vs Ground
Separating what looks like effort from what actually produces a result.

This exercise begins with an honest inventory. Not the version that makes your schedule sound productive. The real one.

Coaching Prompt

Where in your life right now are you putting in real hours, real effort, and real tiredness, that has not produced a result existing outside of your own head?

Not the activity you'd defend if someone questioned it. The one you already suspect, quietly, isn't actually moving anything. Where does that answer land in your body?

Dashboard Numbers — Looks Like Effort
Real Ground — Actually Covered

Notice ThisIf an activity in your dashboard column gets praised by others as responsible, thorough, or smart, that praise does not make it real ground. It just makes the treadmill more comfortable to stay on.

2
The Origin — When Failing Became Dangerous
Tracing the root fear the treadmill has been protecting you from.

Staying on the treadmill is not a discipline problem. It is usually a very reasonable response to a fear that was formed somewhere specific. You cannot dismantle a fear you have not traced.

Coaching Prompt

When did you first learn that failing at something meant something dangerous about who you are, rather than something that simply happens on the way to something working?

Go back to the earliest version of this, not the most recent disappointment. A specific attempt that didn't work, and a specific reaction, from yourself or from someone else, that turned the event into an identity. What comes up when you sit with that honestly?

The Message That Was Sent
The Rule You Built From It

Notice ThisThe rule was written to protect you from a specific pain. It may have made sense at the time. Staying on the treadmill has likely cost you more than that original pain ever did.

3
The Treadmill Named — No Filter
Saying plainly what you've been running on.

In Episode 51, a coaching client discovered that his "research" was a treadmill disguising a fear of failing again after previous businesses hadn't worked. This exercise puts you through the same discovery, in writing.

Coaching Prompt

If you had to say it in one honest sentence, what is your treadmill right now, and what is the real thing it has been letting you avoid?

Not the socially acceptable version. The specific activity, and the specific real action it has been substituting for.

"He wasn't lazy. He was on a treadmill, sweating through hours of research daily, while the real ground never moved an inch closer."
Part Two

Define — Building Your Way Off the Machine

Part Two turns what you named in Part One into a working plan: the specific fear, spoken plainly, and the specific first step that exists outside your own head.

4
Getting Off the Treadmill — Your Working Builder
One page. Your treadmill, your fear, your real-ground equivalent.

This is the working document everything else refers back to. Keep it specific. A vague plan gets absorbed back into the treadmill within days.

Your Treadmill Breakdown

Name It, Trace It, Replace It

5
The Words — Naming the Fear Out Loud
Writing the sentence so it loses power over you.

A fear running quietly in the background of a busy life has far more power than a fear spoken plainly, once, out loud. This exercise drafts the exact sentence.

Coaching Prompt

If you had to say the whole truth in one breath, with no softening, what would you actually say?

Use the structure directly. I am avoiding [the real action] because I am afraid of [the specific fear]. Say it the way you'd say it to a coach, not the way it would sound in a caption.

Read both sentences back out loud once before moving on. Notice which one the fear wants you to believe, and which one your own evidence actually supports.

Part Three

Pressure Test — Testing the Plan Against Real Life

A plan survives easily on paper. It does not survive as easily under praise for staying busy, a setback after stepping off, or a stressful week, unless you have already thought it through. Part Three does that thinking now.

6
Scenario One — The Productive-Looking Excuse
When the treadmill activity gets praised as responsible.
The Scenario

You mention your "research phase" or your "planning process" to a friend or colleague, and they respond with genuine admiration. "That's so smart." "You're being so thorough." The praise feels good, and it quietly reinforces staying exactly where you are.

7
Scenario Two — The Setback After Stepping Off
When the real-ground step doesn't go the way you hoped.
The Scenario

You take the real-ground step. It doesn't go well. The proposal is rejected, the launch gets a quiet response, the conversation is harder than expected. Every instinct says the fear was right all along, and the treadmill was the safer place to be.

Coaching Prompt

If the step doesn't go well, what is the difference between this being evidence that you are a failure, and this being evidence that this particular attempt did not work?

Write the sentence you will say to yourself in that moment, before the old rule from Exercise 2 has a chance to take back over.

8
Scenario Three — The Stressful Week Relapse
When life gets loud and the treadmill feels safest.
The Scenario

A hard week arrives. You're tired, uncertain, low on confidence. The treadmill is right there, familiar and comfortable, and it would be so easy to disappear back into busyness that feels productive without actually risking anything.

Part Four

Protect — Making This a Practice, Not a Moment

One real-ground step is a good start. It is not the whole shift. Part Four turns this worksheet into an ongoing check, so the next treadmill in your life doesn't run quietly for months before you notice it.

9
The Weekly Ground Check — Building the Habit
The simple, recurring practice that catches the next treadmill early.

The goal is not to get off one treadmill once. The goal is to build the habit of noticing quickly when effort has quietly stopped producing real ground.

  • Within 7 Days: Take the real-ground step you named in Exercise 4, by the deadline you set, whether or not the fear has fully gone away.
  • The Day After: Write down what actually happened, and compare it honestly to what the fear predicted would happen.
  • Every Week: Ask yourself one question. "What have I been calling progress this week that hasn't actually moved anything?" Name it if it exists.
  • Every Month: Revisit your Dashboard Audit from Exercise 1. Has anything moved from real ground back into a dashboard number? Update it honestly.
  • Whenever Praise Arrives for Busyness: Run the check from Exercise 6 before letting the compliment extend your stay on the machine.

Before You Close This

The client who inspired this episode wasn't lazy or unmotivated. He had real skill and a real vision. He was simply running hard enough, on a machine designed to never let him arrive, that he could tell himself he was working while never having to risk the thing that actually frightened him.

I've lived a version of this too. The treadmill is comfortable precisely because it never asks you to risk anything real. That comfort is also exactly what keeps you standing in the same spot for years at a time.

The fear does not have to disappear before you take the step. It only has to stop being the only voice deciding what you do next.

Living in Full Expression means covering real distance, not just producing real sweat, even when the ground outside feels far less certain than the machine.

Before you close this session — write the one thing you now know, that you did not know when you opened it.

The machine will always be there, comfortable and familiar. You now know exactly what it's been protecting you from, exactly what the first real step looks like, and exactly what to say to yourself if it doesn't go perfectly. The only thing left is the seven days.
