Before You Begin

How to Use This Worksheet

This worksheet is built to be used, not just read. You will audit three real Nos, not one, because a single example rarely breaks a pattern. You will leave with two actual scripts, words you can send or say, not just insight about what you should eventually do.

Part One is the inventory. Part Two is where you run the two-bucket test on your most significant No and build your actual response. Part Three pressure tests that response against the situations most likely to derail it. Part Four turns this into a permanent habit for every future No.

Use real examples. Real names, even if only for your own eyes. Vague entries produce vague clarity. Specific entries produce an actual shift.

🧭 Recalculating — Hold This Throughout

A GPS never questions whether you should be driving when you miss a turn. It says one word, recalculating, and gives you a new route based on where you actually are.

This worksheet is your recalculation. Not an apology tour for the Nos you've received. A practical route forward, built from what they actually mean.

What This Session Will Produce
  • Something named — three specific Nos you're still carrying, separated from the stories you attached to them
  • Something tested — an honest, two-bucket read on your most significant No: about them, or about your approach
  • Something scripted — the actual words to ask someone why, and the actual words to release a No that was never about you
  • Something committed — one specific adjustment or re-engagement, with a deadline, built from real feedback rather than assumption
Part One

Discover — The No Inventory

Before you can recalculate anything, you need the actual data. This part builds a real inventory of the Nos currently shaping how you show up.

1
The No Inventory — Three Real Examples
Naming three specific Nos you're still carrying, not just one.

Most people can only name the one No that hurt the most. This exercise asks for three, because patterns don't show up in a single data point.

Coaching Prompt

Think of three separate times someone said no to something you offered, proposed, or asked for, whether in business, relationships, or elsewhere. Not the vaguest ones. The ones you can still recall clearly.

For each one, capture who said it, what they actually said, and what you privately concluded it meant about you.

Fact vs Story — The Split
Choosing your most significant No and separating it cleanly.

From your three, choose the one with the strongest grip on you right now. The rest of this worksheet focuses on that one.

Coaching Prompt

Looking only at the literal words that were said, what does the No actually prove, with no interpretation added?

Usually, almost nothing beyond "not right now" or "not this." Compare that to the much larger story you likely built on top of it.

Did I Ever Actually Ask?
Checking whether your conclusion was ever confirmed.

Most people build an entire story about a No without ever confirming it with the person who said it. This exercise checks that directly.

"If you never asked and simply assumed the worst, that is not a personal failing. That is simply the default most of us were never taught to question."
Part Two

Define — Running the Two-Bucket Test

Part Two takes your chosen No through the actual test, and builds the exact words you'll use, whether you're releasing it or acting on it.

4
The Two-Bucket Test
Deciding honestly which bucket this No actually belongs in.

Every No belongs in one of two buckets. This exercise makes you choose, based on evidence, not feeling.

Coaching Prompt

Based on what you actually know, confirmed or reasonably inferred, was this No primarily about their circumstances, timing, or capacity, or was it primarily about your approach, offer, or presentation?

If you genuinely don't know, that's useful information too, and points you toward Part Three's script for asking.

5
Your Two Ready Scripts
The actual words for releasing it, or for adjusting and re-engaging.

Insight without language rarely survives contact with the next uncomfortable moment. This exercise builds both scripts so you have exact words ready, whichever bucket applies.

Script One — If It Was About Them: The Release Statement

Say this to yourself, out loud, when the old story tries to resurface.

Script Two — If It Was About Your Approach: The Adjustment Statement

Name the specific, correctable change, then commit to re-engaging.

Part Three

Pressure Test — Testing This Against Real Situations

The two-bucket test survives easily on paper. It gets harder around people you love, after a streak of Nos, and in the actual moment of asking someone why. Part Three prepares you for all three.

6
Scenario One — When the No Comes From Someone Close
Why a No from someone who should say yes hurts more, and what to do about it.
The Scenario

Someone close to you, whose relationship with you should make agreement easier, says no to something you were genuinely convinced would help them. It stings in a way a stranger's No never quite does.

7
Scenario Two — A Streak of Nos in a Row
When multiple rejections compound faster than you can process them individually.
The Scenario

You get several Nos close together. Even with the two-bucket test in hand, the sheer volume starts to feel like evidence on its own, a pattern too large to just be individual circumstances.

Coaching Prompt

Have you actually run each of these Nos through the two-bucket test individually, or have you let the total number substitute for real analysis?

A streak feels like a verdict. It is still just a set of individual data points, each one deserving its own honest read.

8
Scenario Three — Actually Asking Why
The exact words for a question most people never ask.
The Scenario

You want to know the real reason behind a No, but asking feels awkward, vulnerable, or like it might make things worse. So the question stays unasked, and the assumption stays unconfirmed.

Script Three — Asking Why, Gracefully

Adjust the specifics, but keep the tone: low-pressure, genuinely curious, easy to decline.

Part Four

Protect — Making This Automatic

One recalculated No is a good start. It is not the whole shift. Part Four turns the two-bucket test into a habit you run automatically, every time, going forward.

9
The Ongoing No Log — Building the Habit
The simple, recurring practice that keeps every future No from becoming a verdict.

The goal is not to fix three old Nos and stop. The goal is to build a reflex that runs the two-bucket test on every new No, quickly, before a story has time to calcify.

  • Within 24 Hours of Any New No: Write the literal words, separately from your first emotional reaction, using the same split from Exercise 2.
  • Within 48 Hours: Run the two-bucket test. Ask directly if appropriate, using the script from Exercise 8.
  • Within 7 Days: Either use the Release Statement or the Adjustment Statement from Exercise 5, and take the next action, whichever bucket applies.
  • Every Month: Revisit your No Inventory from Exercise 1. Are the same harsh phrases still repeating? Update honestly.
  • After Any Streak: Use your plan from Exercise 7 before letting volume substitute for individual analysis.

Before You Close This

Some of the coaching clients who said no to me genuinely wanted what I was offering. They just couldn't afford it at that time. If I had accepted my first interpretation, that the No meant I wasn't good enough, I would have carried a false story into every offer that came after.

Your GPS never questions whether you should be driving when you miss a turn. It recalculates, and gives you the next route based on where you actually are.

Living in Full Expression means hearing "No" as feedback, and refusing to let it become a verdict you never actually earned.

Before you close this session — write the one thing you now know, that you did not know when you opened it.

Another No is coming. That was always going to be true. You now have the inventory process, the two-bucket test, and two ready scripts, so the next one gets sorted instead of absorbed as a verdict.

Recalculate, and keep driving.
