Before You Begin

How to Use This Worksheet

This is the coaching session the episode prepared you for — in written form, with room to go further than a single listening allows. Four parts. Nine exercises. One frustration named honestly, weighed accurately, and moved by the end of it.

The parts move in sequence for a reason. Part One is where you name what's actually frustrating you and get honest about how much of your energy has gone into blame instead of action. Part Two turns that honesty into a real plan. Part Three pressure tests the plan against the exact moments most likely to stall it. Part Four makes this a habit, not a one-time fridge move.

Write what is actually true, not the version that makes the delay sound reasonable. Naming who or what you've been blaming is not about guilt. It's about seeing clearly where your energy has actually been going, so you can redirect it.

🧊 The Fridge — Hold This Throughout

A loaf of bread fell behind a refrigerator. Moving the fridge wasn't optional, because the bread was needed for breakfast, and left there, it would spoil, smell, and draw insects. Behind it was also a lost spoon, given up on days earlier, and a layer of dust that had been building for a long time.

This worksheet is how you find out exactly what's behind your own fridge, whether it's genuinely bread or something that can wait, and what you might discover once you finally move it.

What This Session Will Produce
  • Something named — the specific frustration currently sitting in your life, stated plainly instead of vaguely
  • Something weighed — an honest read on whether this is bread, with real consequences, or something that can genuinely wait
  • Something traced — where your energy has actually been going: toward blame and wallowing, or toward the action itself
  • Something committed — one specific action, with a deadline, that finally moves the fridge this week
Part One

Discover — Naming What's Actually Behind It

Before you can move anything, you need to see clearly what's frustrating you, how much it actually matters, and where your energy has really been going.

1
The Fridge Audit
Naming the current frustrations you've mostly just been irritated by.

This exercise begins with an honest inventory. Not the general complaint. The specific, actual frustration.

Coaching Prompt

What is the current frustration, disappointment, or delay sitting in your life right now, the one you've mostly just been irritated by, without asking what it might actually be pointing you toward?

Name it specifically. Not "things haven't been going my way." The actual event, the actual person, the actual delay.

The Frustration, Named Specifically
What I've Concluded About It So Far

Notice ThisIf your conclusion is a verdict about yourself or your life in general, rather than a specific read on this one event, that's often a sign the story has grown bigger than the actual situation.

2
The Weight Test — Bread or Something Small
Deciding honestly how urgently this actually needs your action.

Not every frustration deserves the same urgency. This exercise is where you decide, honestly, which kind you're dealing with.

Coaching Prompt

If you leave this exactly where it is, what genuinely happens? Does it spoil, compound, or cost you more the longer it sits? Or can it actually wait without real harm?

Be honest rather than dramatic. Not everything is bread. But some things genuinely are, and pretending otherwise is its own kind of avoidance.

🔴 If This Is Bread
🟢 If This Can Genuinely Wait

Notice ThisIf you're not sure which category this falls into, ask what happens after one more week of leaving it exactly as it is. The answer usually tells you.

3
The Blame and Wallow Check
Tracing where your energy has actually been going.

Blame and wallowing feel active. In practice, they are often a sophisticated way of delaying the action the moment is actually asking for. This exercise names both plainly.

Coaching Prompt

Who or what have you been blaming for this frustration, and how much energy has that actually cost you? Have you also been wallowing, replaying the disappointment rather than acting on it?

This is not about guilt. It's about seeing clearly where the energy has gone, so you can redirect it toward the actual task.

"Every minute spent deciding whose fault the bread falling was, was a minute the bread spent sitting behind a dusty fridge, getting closer to spoiling."
Part Two

Define — Building Your Plan to Move It

Part Two turns what you named in Part One into a specific plan, with real words attached, for finally moving the fridge.

4
Move the Fridge — Your Working Builder
One page. The frustration, the stakes, and the required action.

This is the working document everything else refers back to. Keep it specific. A vague intention to "deal with it eventually" rarely becomes an actual Tuesday.

Your Move the Fridge Plan

Name It, Weigh It, Move It

5
The Words — Separating the Event From the Story
Drafting the sentence that reframes the frustration as a prompt.

The event and the meaning you've assigned to it are two separate things. This exercise puts that separation into words you can return to.

Coaching Prompt

If you had to say it in one breath, what is the sentence that reminds you this frustration might be a prompt, not a punishment?

Write it the way you would actually say it to yourself in the moment of irritation, not the way it would sound on a poster.

Read it back out loud once before moving on. If it steadies you rather than just sounding nice, it's ready.

Part Three

Pressure Test — Testing the Plan Against Real Life

A plan survives easily in writing. It does not survive as easily against real reluctance, a returning blame reflex, or a disappointing result, unless you've already thought it through.

6
Scenario One — When You Genuinely Don't Feel Like It
When the action feels heavier than you expected, even though it matters.
The Scenario

The deadline from Exercise 4 arrives, and you genuinely don't feel like doing the work. It's inconvenient, tiring, or simply unpleasant, even though you know the stakes are real.

7
Scenario Two — The Blame Reflex Returns
When irritation flares up again mid-action.
The Scenario

You're in the middle of taking the required action, and the old irritation resurfaces. You catch yourself, once again, mentally assigning blame instead of simply continuing the task.

Coaching Prompt

What is the shortest, simplest thing you can say to yourself to notice the blame reflex and return to the task without needing to fully resolve the irritation first?

You don't need to stop feeling frustrated. You only need to keep moving despite it.

8
Scenario Three — Nothing Significant Turns Up
When moving the fridge doesn't reveal a spoon.
The Scenario

You take the action. You move the fridge. And this time, there's no lost spoon waiting behind it, just the bread you needed, and maybe a bit of a mess. It would be easy to feel like the lesson didn't apply this time.

Part Four

Protect — Making This a Practice, Not a Moment

One moved fridge is a good start. It is not the whole shift. Part Four turns this worksheet into an ongoing practice, so the next frustration gets read for its message quickly, instead of sitting in blame for weeks.

9
The Weekly Fridge Check — Building the Habit
The simple, recurring practice that catches frustration early next time.

The goal is not to move one fridge and stop. The goal is to build the habit of quickly asking what a frustration might be pointing toward, before blame and wallowing eat the time that action needs.

  • Within 7 Days: Take the required action named in Exercise 4, by the deadline you set, whether or not you feel like it.
  • The Day After: Write down what actually happened, and whether anything unexpected turned up behind it.
  • Every Week: Ask yourself one question. "What's currently frustrating me that I've been blaming or wallowing over instead of weighing and acting on?"
  • Every Month: Revisit your Fridge Audit from Exercise 1. Has a new frustration surfaced that deserves the same honest weighing?
  • Whenever Blame Resurfaces: Use the one-line redirect from Exercise 7 before letting irritation cost you more time than the task itself.

Before You Close This

Neither of us felt like moving that fridge. Blaming each other felt easier, in the moment, than doing the actual work. But the bread was going to spoil regardless of how justified the irritation felt.

What we found behind it, a spoon we'd given up on and a mess we hadn't known we'd been ignoring, wasn't guaranteed. We didn't move the fridge because we expected a reward. We moved it because the bread mattered, and the discovery came anyway.

Living in Full Expression means learning to read frustration for its message, not just its inconvenience, and having the discipline to act on it instead of losing days to blame and wallowing.

Before you close this session — write the one thing you now know, that you did not know when you opened it.

The fridge will still feel heavy to move. That was never going to change. You now know exactly what's behind it, exactly how urgently it needs your attention, and exactly what you'll say to yourself when you don't feel like acting. The only thing left is the seven days.
